Walk into ten different sound baths and you might have ten different experiences. One is a single gong pushing air through your chest. Another is a soft wash of crystal bowls in a candlelit studio. A third happens on a paddleboard, or in a conference room during a lunch break. The phrase "sound bath" covers all of it, which is part of why newcomers get confused before they ever book a session.

The variation comes down to three things: which instruments are used, how the session is structured, and where it happens. A gong session and a crystal bowl session share a name but almost nothing else in how they feel. This guide maps the full landscape so you can figure out which format actually fits what you're looking for, whether that's deep release, better sleep, a team offsite, or just curiosity.

Prices below are general ranges pulled from typical studio and independent practitioner rates. Actual cost depends heavily on your city, the practitioner's experience, and whether you're booking a public class or a private session.

## Gong Bath

A gong bath centers on one or more large gongs, sometimes paired with smaller supporting instruments like chimes or a frame drum. The sound is physical. Gongs produce low frequencies and complex overtones that you feel in your sternum and stomach as much as you hear them. Practitioners build waves of intensity, from a near-silent opening to a full wall of sound, then bring it back down.

This format suits people who want a strong, embodied experience rather than something gentle. It's not the best entry point if you're sound-sensitive or new to loud environments. Sessions typically run 60 to 90 minutes and cost $25 to $45 for a group class, more for private bookings. For a full breakdown of how gong baths differ from other formats, see our gong bath guide.

## Crystal Bowl Sound Bath

Crystal singing bowls are made of quartz and produce a clear, sustained tone when played with a mallet around the rim. Each bowl is tuned to a note, and practitioners often build sequences that move through several bowls at once, layering tones rather than building volume.

Crystal bowl sessions tend to feel brighter and more meditative than gong-heavy formats. They're a common choice for people focused on stress reduction or a calming close to the week. Expect 45 to 75 minutes and a price range of $20 to $40 for a group session. We compare this format directly against Tibetan bowls in a separate guide if you're deciding between the two.

## Tibetan Bowl Sound Bath

Tibetan singing bowls are metal, often a blend of several metals, and produce a warmer, more textured tone than crystal bowls. They're played by striking the bowl or circling a mallet around the rim, and many practitioners rest them directly on or near the body during part of the session.

This format has a longer history in wellness practice and tends to draw people already familiar with meditation or yoga. Sessions run 45 to 90 minutes, priced similarly to crystal bowl classes. Our crystal vs. Tibetan bowl guide covers the practical differences in more depth.

## Voice and Overtone Singing Bath

Some practitioners use only the human voice, chanting or producing overtones without any instruments at all. This format leans on breath, resonance, and sometimes group toning, where the audience participates by humming or chanting alongside the leader.

It suits people drawn to vocal or breathwork practices, and it can feel more intimate than an instrument-based session since there's no equipment between the practitioner and the group. Sessions run 30 to 60 minutes and are less common as standalone offerings, more often folded into a broader sound healing class. Pricing tracks with other formats, roughly $20 to $35.

## Brass and Alchemy Bowl Bath

Alchemy bowls are a newer category, made from quartz fused with metals or minerals, marketed with claims about specific frequencies or intentions tied to each blend. Brass bowls and bells add another metallic texture, often used as accents within a broader gong or crystal session rather than as the sole instrument.

These formats appeal to people already comfortable with sound healing who want to explore instrument variety. They're harder to find as a dedicated class and more often appear in workshops or practitioner training sessions. Cost varies widely, from $25 group rates to $150+ for specialty workshops.

## Floating Sound Bath

A floating sound bath happens on water, usually in a heated pool with foam mats or supported flotation devices. The sound source, often crystal bowls or a smaller gong, plays from the pool deck while participants float on their backs, letting the water handle the work of holding them still.

This is one of the more physically distinct formats on this list, since the water itself changes how sound and relaxation register in the body. It's popular in spa and wellness resort settings and tends to run 60 minutes for $45 to $90, reflecting the added logistics of a pool space. ## Private Sound Bath

A private or one-on-one sound bath is any of the formats above, delivered to a single person or small group rather than a public class. Practitioners can adjust instrument choice, pacing, and intensity to the individual, and some incorporate light touch or bodywork.

This format works well for people managing anxiety, grief, or a specific health concern who want a tailored session, or for couples and small friend groups booking something private. Expect to pay considerably more than a group class, typically $100 to $300 per session depending on location and practitioner experience. ## Outdoor Sound Bath

Outdoor sessions take a standard format, most often gong or crystal bowl, and move it into a park, forest, beach, or rooftop. The appeal is layering ambient outdoor sound, wind, birds, distant traffic, into the session rather than isolating it in a studio.

This suits people who find indoor group classes too closed-in, or who want to combine sound healing with time outside. Sessions are typically seasonal, priced close to standard group classes at $20 to $40, sometimes free at community events or festivals.

## Corporate and Workplace Sound Bath

Some practitioners specialize in bringing sound baths into offices, usually as part of a wellness day, offsite, or team-building event. Sessions are shorter than a typical class, often 20 to 30 minutes, and scaled down to fit a conference room or open office space.

This format is booked by the employer rather than the individual, and pricing reflects that: practitioners typically charge $150 to $500+ per session depending on group size and location, not a per-person rate.

## Choosing Between Formats

If you're new to sound baths, a group crystal or Tibetan bowl class is the easiest entry point. It's gentler than a gong bath and more widely available than floating or private sessions. From there, most people either deepen into gong work for a stronger physical experience or move toward private sessions for something more personalized.

Think about what you're actually trying to solve for. If you want something you can drop into after work with no planning, a standard group class in a nearby studio covers that. If you're dealing with a specific issue like chronic anxiety or trouble sleeping, a private session gives a practitioner room to adjust pacing and instrument choice to you directly, rather than a room full of strangers with different needs. If your goal is a team activity, look specifically for a practitioner who lists corporate or workplace sessions, since not every studio-based teacher offers that format.

It's also worth trying more than one type before deciding you don't like sound baths. Someone who finds a gong session overwhelming might find a crystal bowl class calming, and someone who finds bowls a bit flat might respond strongly to a gong's physical intensity. The instrument matters as much as the format.
