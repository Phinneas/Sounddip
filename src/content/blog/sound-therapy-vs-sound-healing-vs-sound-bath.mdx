Sound therapy is a clinical practice, often used by occupational or speech therapists to support specific health goals. Sound healing is the broader wellness umbrella that covers sound therapy, meditation, and any practice using sound to shift mood or nervous system state. A sound bath is one specific format within sound healing: a live or recorded session, usually in a group, where instruments like singing bowls and gongs wash over you while you lie down and listen. If you only remember one thing, remember this: sound bath is the format, sound healing is the category, and sound therapy is the clinical branch.

These three terms get used interchangeably online, which causes real confusion when you're trying to book something or understand what a practitioner actually offers. Here's what separates them, where they cross over, and which one you're probably looking for.

## What Is Sound Therapy

Sound therapy usually shows up in clinical or rehabilitative settings. Speech-language pathologists use auditory training as part of therapy for processing disorders. Occupational therapists sometimes use specific frequencies or rhythmic sound as a regulation tool for sensory processing differences. Some audiologists use sound therapy protocols to treat tinnitus, retraining the brain to filter out a persistent ringing.

The word "therapy" signals a few things: a practitioner with clinical training, a treatment plan tied to a diagnosis or goal, and outcomes that get measured over time. Sound therapy is not usually a single drop-in session. It's closer to physical therapy: structured, goal-oriented, and typically billed through a healthcare provider rather than a wellness studio.

## What Is Sound Healing

Sound healing is the umbrella term. It covers any practice that uses sound intentionally to support relaxation, emotional release, or a shift in mental state. Under that umbrella you'll find sound baths, chanting practices like kirtan, tuning fork sessions, binaural beat recordings, and yes, some forms of sound therapy too.

Sound healing doesn't require a clinical credential. Many practitioners train through certification programs (see our guide on [what sound bath certifications actually mean](/blog/sound-bath-certifications/)), but the field isn't regulated the way clinical therapy is. This is both the appeal and the catch: sound healing is accessible and low-barrier, but the quality and training behind it varies a lot from one practitioner to the next.

If someone describes themselves as a "sound healer," they're telling you their general orientation, not the specific format of what they do. That's where sound bath comes in.

## What Is a Sound Bath

A sound bath is a specific, bookable format. You lie down, usually on a mat with a blanket, and a practitioner plays instruments, most often crystal or Tibetan singing bowls, gongs, chimes, and sometimes voice, for 45 minutes to an hour. There's no dancing, no chanting along, no active participation. You just listen. For a full breakdown of what a first session looks like, see our guide to [what a sound bath actually is](/blog/what-is-a-sound-bath/).

Sound baths can be public group events at a studio, private sessions booked for one person or a small group, or outdoor sessions tied to seasonal or community events. The instruments matter here in a way they don't for the broader "sound healing" label. If you want to understand what you're actually listening to, our guide to [sound bath instruments](/blog/sound-bath-music/) breaks down what each one does and why practitioners choose them.

Because "sound bath" describes a concrete, repeatable experience, it's also the easiest of the three terms to search for and book. That's a big part of why it has become the most common entry point into sound healing generally.

## Where They Overlap and Where They Split

All three terms share a root idea: sound, used deliberately, changes how you feel. Past that, they split on regulation, structure, and setting.

Sound therapy is the only one of the three that typically involves a licensed clinician and a treatment goal. Sound healing is the broad category with no licensing requirement and wide variation in training. Sound bath is a specific event format that sits inside the sound healing category and is what most people mean when they say they want to "try sound healing" for the first time.

A tuning fork session used by an audiologist for tinnitus is sound therapy. A drop-in gong bath at a local studio is a sound bath, and by extension, sound healing. A weekly chanting circle is sound healing, but not a sound bath, since there's no bowls-and-gongs listening format involved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a sound bath the same as sound healing?

A sound bath is one format within sound healing. Sound healing is the broader category; sound bath refers specifically to a session where you lie down and listen to instruments like singing bowls and gongs.

### Is sound therapy covered by insurance?

Sometimes, when it's delivered by a licensed clinician as part of a documented treatment plan, such as tinnitus retraining therapy through an audiologist. Sound baths and general sound healing sessions are typically not covered, since they're wellness services rather than clinical treatment.

### Do I need training to lead a sound bath?

No license is required, but reputable practitioners usually complete a certification program covering instrument technique, session structure, and basic safety. Ask a practitioner about their training before booking, especially for group sessions.

### Which one should I try first?

If you want a clinical outcome, like help with tinnitus or sensory processing, ask a doctor about sound therapy. If you just want to feel more relaxed and see what the practice is about, a sound bath is the easiest and most accessible entry point.
