Not all sound baths are equal. That is not a criticism of the field so much as a description of it: sound healing has no licensing board, no state exam, and no governing body that can revoke anyone's right to practice. Anyone can buy a set of bowls, post a listing, and take your money. Most practitioners are genuine and careful. Some are not. The difference is usually visible before you book, if you know what to look at.

These seven criteria separate a well-run session from a disappointing one. None require expertise. All can be checked in a few minutes.

## 1. Training and certification background

Look for a named training background rather than a vague claim. "Certified sound healer" means very little on its own, because the certification can come from a weekend workshop or a hundred-hour program — and the phrase alone does not tell you which. What matters is whether the practitioner names where they trained, how long the training ran, and what it covered.

You are looking for a school or teacher's name, a program length, and evidence the training covered more than striking a bowl. Real programs include acoustics, session design, contraindications, and working with people who have health conditions. Someone trained only in technique and not in who should not be in the room is a risk, however good they sound.

If their background is genuinely self-taught, that is not automatically disqualifying — but they should say so plainly. Vagueness is the red flag, not the absence of a certificate. Our guide to [sound bath certifications](/blog/sound-bath-certifications/) breaks down what the major programs actually cover.

## 2. Instrument quality

Authentic bowls and cheap imports behave differently, and it shows up in the session. Mass-produced bowls sold as "antique Tibetan" are often inconsistent in shape and rim thickness, producing uneven tone and a strike that will not hold cleanly. That does not make them dangerous. It makes the experience weaker and less controlled.

Ask what they play and where it came from. A practitioner who can tell you their bowls are hand-hammered Tibetan or quartz crystal has thought about their instrument as an instrument rather than a prop. A skilled practitioner with poor instruments can only do so much. Our breakdown of [every type of sound bath](/blog/every-type-of-sound-bath-explained/) covers what each instrument family does.

## 3. Group size

Smaller groups mean a more personalised session, and the difference is practical. In a room of six, the practitioner can place instruments closer to you, watch how the group responds, and adjust as they go. In a room of thirty, you get a fixed set played to the middle of the room.

Neither is wrong, and a large group session is often the right way to start. But if you are booking something expensive or you have specific reasons for being there, a smaller group is worth paying for. Ask for the cap, not just the format.

## 4. Whether they screen for contraindications

This is the criterion most people skip and the one that matters most. A practitioner who asks about your health history before a session is doing their job. Pacemakers, pregnancy, epilepsy, tinnitus, recent surgery, and acute injuries all change where instruments go and how loud they get.

Ask directly: do you ask attendees about health conditions beforehand? The answer should be yes without hesitation. If they place bowls on the body, that question matters more still. A practitioner who has never thought about this has never thought about what can go wrong — that is inexperience, and it is disqualifying regardless. Our guide to [sound bath negative effects](/blog/sound-bath-negative-effects/) covers what can actually go wrong.

## 5. Communication style before the session

What happens before the sound starts tells you most of what you need to know. Do they explain what to expect, how long the session runs, and what to do if you feel overwhelmed partway through? Or do they hand you a mat and start playing?

There is no single correct style — some practitioners talk constantly, others stay silent from the first strike. But silence is different from leaving you uninformed. A short orientation at the start is standard practice, and its absence usually predicts other gaps. This is also your read on whether you can talk to them at all: if a question before the session gets a defensive response, that matters more than any number of five-star reviews.

## 6. Reviews from real attendees

Read reviews critically and for detail. A long list of "amazing experience!!" tells you almost nothing. A review that describes the room, the pacing, or how the practitioner handled someone who was uncomfortable tells you a great deal.

Look for patterns rather than scores. If multiple people mention the practitioner checking in before and after, that is a signal. If several mention feeling rushed or no one explaining anything, believe them. Negative reviews with specifics are more useful than positive ones without.

Reviews also will not tell you about clinical outcomes. Be wary of any practitioner whose testimonials claim to heal conditions.

## 7. Whether they offer an intro call or trial

The best signal of a confident practitioner is willingness to talk before you pay. A free intro call, a short trial session, or simply answering detailed questions over email all show someone who expects to be evaluated rather than trusted on faith.

This is especially worth seeking out before a private session, which costs substantially more than a group class. A practitioner charging $150 or more for an hour who will not spend ten minutes on the phone with you first is telling you what the relationship will be like.

Not every good practitioner offers this — some run busy group schedules and genuinely cannot. But if you ask and get nothing back, that is information too.
